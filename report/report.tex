\section*{Overview}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Overview}
Each of the 87 respondents is a node. We connect two people when their survey
answers are very similar. We then do three sets of analysis on this network.
First, we measure the structure. This covers degree, clustering, components,
centrality, and communities. Second, we run two dynamic processes from the
course. These are the DeGroot opinion average and an SIS contagion. Third, we run
two null models. These test whether the structure is more than chance. This report
defines each concept in a shaded box before it uses the concept.

\section{Dataset Documentation}

\textbf{The raw survey.} The survey has 97 responses to 60 opinion statements.
There are 15 statements in each of four topics. A short code marks the topic of
each statement. \textbf{T} is Technology, \textbf{E} is Education, \textbf{S} is
Ethics, and \textbf{V} is Environment. Each statement uses a five-point Likert
scale from \emph{Strongly Disagree} to \emph{Strongly Agree}. The survey also
gives a ``No Comments'' option.

\textbf{Preparation.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
  \item \emph{Likert to number.} We map the scale to a symmetric integer.
  Strongly Disagree is $-2$. Disagree is $-1$. Neutral is $0$. Agree is $+1$.
  Strongly Agree is $+2$. The symmetry makes $0$ a true neutral. A mean score then
  shows a direction and a strength at the same time.
  \item \emph{``No Comments'' is missing, not neutral.} We record it as a blank,
  not as $0$. A refusal to answer is not the same as ``I am neutral.'' There are
  62 blanks out of $87\times60=5220$ ($1.19\%$). They affect 19 respondents.
  \item \emph{Quality filter.} We remove any respondent who answered fewer than
  half of the 60 questions. This removes 10 sparse responses. It leaves
  \textbf{87 respondents}. We use these 87 everywhere below.
  \item \emph{Result.} The cleaned data is an $87\times60$ table of scores in
  $[-2,+2]$, with a blank for each ``No Comments.'' Each question keeps its topic
  and its text.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Missing values.} Two rules are correct, and we use both on purpose. The
structural work must agree with the graph. For this work, we replace a blank with
$0$ before we average. This is the same step that the edge builder uses, so the
opinions and the edges use the same numbers. The descriptive statistics instead
drop the blank from a person's average. The two rules differ by at most $0.01$ on
the class mean. We never compare a number from one rule with a number from the
other.

\section{Pipeline Followed}

\begin{concept}{opinion vector and cosine similarity}
Each respondent becomes a \textbf{vector} of up to 60 numeric answers,
$\mathbf{x}_i\in[-2,2]^{60}$. Blank cells become $0$. The similarity of two people
is the \textbf{cosine} of the angle between their vectors.
\[
  s_{ij}=\cos(\mathbf{x}_i,\mathbf{x}_j)=
  \frac{\mathbf{x}_i\cdot\mathbf{x}_j}{\lVert\mathbf{x}_i\rVert\,\lVert\mathbf{x}_j\rVert}\in[-1,1].
\]
The value is $+1$ when two answer patterns point the same way. It is $0$ when they
are unrelated. It is $-1$ when they are opposite. The cosine rewards a shared
pattern across all 60 questions. It does not reward only a shared average level.
\end{concept}

\textbf{From answers to a network.} A node is a respondent. An edge means that two
people hold similar opinions. We compute the full $87\times87$ cosine-similarity
matrix. We then keep only the \textbf{top 10\% most similar pairs} as edges. This
is the 90th-percentile cut. A hard threshold is necessary. Almost everyone in this
class agrees on average. A loose threshold gives a near-complete graph with no
information. The 90th-percentile rule gives exactly \textbf{375 edges}. We build
\textbf{five networks} with the same rule. One network is \emph{overall} and uses
all 60 questions. Four networks use one topic each with 15 questions. The same
rule means that any difference between the networks comes from the opinions. A
sensitivity sweep in \S\ref{sec:sens} tests the threshold.

the saved networks and never rebuild the graph.

\subsection{Structure of the overall network}

\begin{concept}{degree, density, clustering, components}
The \textbf{degree} $k$ of a node is its number of edges. The \textbf{average
degree} $\langle k\rangle$ is the mean degree of the class. The \textbf{density}
is the fraction of all possible edges that are present. The \textbf{clustering
coefficient} measures how often two neighbours of a node also connect to each
other. Its range is $0$ to $1$. A \textbf{connected component} is a set of nodes
that can all reach each other. The largest one is the \textbf{giant component}.
Inside it, the \textbf{diameter} is the longest shortest path. The \textbf{average
path length} is the mean shortest path. It shows how many steps separate two
connected people.
\end{concept}

Table~\ref{tab:struct} gives these metrics for all five networks. The overall
network is sparse, with a density of $0.10$. It is dense where it connects, with a
clustering of $0.41$. The connected people form tight triangles. 36 of the 87
respondents are \textbf{isolates}. An isolate has no strong-similarity partner.
The giant component is compact. Two connected people are only about $1.9$ steps
apart.

\subsection{Central people, and how the network splits}

\begin{concept}{four centralities}
\textbf{Degree} centrality counts direct connections. \textbf{Closeness}
centrality measures how short the average path is from a node to all others.
\textbf{Betweenness} centrality counts how often a node lies on the shortest path
between two others. It shows the value of a node as a bridge. \textbf{Eigenvector}
centrality gives more weight to a node that connects to other important nodes.
Degree, closeness, and eigenvector reward a position in the core. Betweenness
rewards a position between groups.
\end{concept}

Degree, closeness, and eigenvector centrality pick almost the same five people
(114, 67, 94, 105, 110). All five score above the class mean on every topic. They
are the uniform strong-agreement answerers. Their 60-value vectors are similar to
many others. Betweenness centrality gives a different answer. Its top node is 41.
Respondent 41 has a maximum Technology score but only an average Education score.
This mixed profile makes 41 a bridge between sub-groups
(Figure~\ref{fig:structure}, right). The most connected person and the best bridge
are not the same person. One centrality is not enough.

\begin{concept}{community, modularity, Louvain}
A \textbf{community} is a group of nodes with many edges inside and few edges to
the outside. \textbf{Modularity} $Q$ scores a given grouping. It measures how many
more edges fall inside the groups than a random graph with the same degrees gives.
Its range is $-\tfrac12$ to $1$. The \textbf{Louvain} algorithm looks for the
grouping with the highest $Q$. A higher $Q$ means clearer groups.
\end{concept}

\subsection{Cohesion tracks opinion \emph{variance}, not opinion strength}

The per-topic numbers go against the raw averages. Ethics ($+1.28$) and
Environment ($+1.37$) have the strongest average agreement. But they have the most
broken networks, with a giant component near $52\%$ and $51\%$. Technology
($+0.99$) and Education ($+0.91$) agree less on average. But they stay the best
connected, at $75\%$ and $72\%$. The cause is the spread, not the mean. Cosine
similarity depends on the full pattern across the 15 questions of a topic.
Technology and Education opinions are tightly grouped, with a standard deviation of
$0.37$ and $0.35$. So most vectors are similar and the graph stays connected.
Ethics and Environment opinions are strong but more spread out, with a standard
deviation of $0.45$ and $0.47$. So the same cut breaks the class into fragments.
The statement ``the class agrees more on Environment than on Technology'' holds for
the raw average. It fails for the network.

\subsection{Dynamics on the network}

\begin{concept}{DeGroot opinion average and stability}
Give each respondent a start opinion, which is their mean score. At each step, each
respondent changes their opinion to the mean of the opinions of their neighbours.
All respondents do this at the same time. The rule is
$\mathbf{x}(t{+}1)=D^{-1}A\,\mathbf{x}(t)$, where $A$ is the adjacency matrix and
$D$ is the degree matrix. This is the \textbf{DeGroot model}. The
\textbf{eigenvalues} of the update matrix control the result. The course rule says
that a linear map is stable when the relevant eigenvalue has a magnitude below $1$.
The \textbf{SLEM} (second-largest eigenvalue modulus) sets the speed. The
\textbf{algebraic connectivity} $\lambda_2=1-\mu_2$ measures how well a component
connects. A larger $\lambda_2$ gives a faster consensus.
\end{concept}

The overall network has 37 components. So there is no single class consensus. The
connected core of 51 people reaches \textbf{1.409} in \textbf{23 steps}. The 36
isolates never move, because an isolate has no neighbour to average with. The
convergence speed follows the algebraic connectivity exactly for all five networks
(Table~\ref{tab:degroot}). This confirms the course stability rule on real data.
The edge weights have almost no effect. The weighted consensus is $1.4099$. The
unweighted consensus is $1.4085$. Row normalisation removes the small $0.85$ to
$0.95$ range of the kept similarities.

\begin{concept}{SIS epidemic threshold}
Model a strong opinion as a contagion. A person is \emph{infected} or
\emph{susceptible}. An infected person infects each neighbour at rate $\beta$. An
infected person recovers at rate $\gamma$. The course result gives a critical ratio
$(\beta/\gamma)_c = 1/\lambda_{\max}$, where $\lambda_{\max}$ is the largest
eigenvalue of the adjacency matrix. A denser core gives a larger $\lambda_{\max}$.
A larger $\lambda_{\max}$ gives a lower threshold. A lower threshold means that an
opinion spreads more easily.
\end{concept}

\subsection{Does the 10\% cut-off matter?}
\label{sec:sens}

We apply the same rule at every quantile from $0.50$ to $0.99$
(Figure~\ref{fig:dynamics}, bottom). Two results hold. First, the topic order is
stable at every cut. Technology stays above Environment. Education stays above
Ethics. This order is a property of the data, not of the 90th percentile. Second,
the isolates are only partly an artifact. Their count falls from 36 at $q{=}0.90$
to 8 at $q{=}0.50$. It never reaches zero. Eight people stay below the median
similarity to everyone. A looser cut also raises the clustering to $0.75$ and makes
the graph half complete. On such a graph, community and centrality lose their
meaning. So $q{=}0.90$ is a tested compromise, not an assumption.

\section{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:results}

\textbf{What the network shows about class opinions.} This class is agreeable. The
mean opinion is positive on all four topics. The raw data shows no opposite camps.
The network shows structure that the averages hide.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=3pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{No global consensus, and the drop-outs are the moderates.} Under
DeGroot the 51-person core agrees at $1.409$. The 36 isolates never move. The core
started at a mean of $1.309$. The isolates started at $0.872$. So the average
process removes the more moderate half of the class. This is a structural
echo-chamber effect. The averaging model does not cause it.

\item \textbf{Cohesion is a variance story.} Section~\ref{sec:analysis} shows that
Technology and Education look more connected than Ethics and Environment. This holds
although their scores are lower. The cause is the smaller spread. Raw agreement is
not the same as social cohesion.

\item \textbf{Spread is easiest where the class is most broken.} Environment and
Ethics have the lowest epidemic thresholds, at $1/\lambda_{\max}=0.042$ and
$0.044$. But they are the least connected topics (Table~\ref{tab:epi}). The edge
count is fixed at 375. So a smaller giant component packs almost the same edges into
fewer people. This raises $\lambda_{\max}$. Fragmentation and easy spread are not
opposites here. This is a real property. It is also a warning about the fixed-edge
method.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{concept}{null models and the z-score}
A null model answers one question. Does a random network with no opinion structure
give the same value? The \textbf{Erdős--Rényi} (ER) model keeps only the number of
edges. It places them at random. The \textbf{configuration model} is stricter. It
keeps the exact degree of each person. It only changes who connects to whom. The
\textbf{z-score} is $z=(\text{real}-\text{null mean})/\text{null s.d.}$ It shows how
far the real value is from the random values. A value of $|z|<2$ means that the real
value looks random.
\end{concept}

\textbf{The structure is in the degree sequence} (Figure~\ref{fig:null}). Against
500 ER graphs the network is unusual on every measure. The clustering z-score is
$+27$ and the giant-fraction z-score is $-362$. Against 500 degree-preserving
rewirings the network is ordinary. The clustering, the giant component, and the
modularity all fall inside $|z|<2$. So once you fix the number of edges of each
person, chance gives the rest of the structure. For this construction, this is the
same statement as ``the connected core is the group that agrees with everything.''
The level of agreement sets the degree. After you fix the degrees, nothing is left
to explain. This does not weaken the structural analysis. It gives the reason for
it.

\begin{concept}{permutation test and p-value}
This test asks whether the communities are more similar in opinion than chance
gives. It shuffles the community labels 10{,}000 times. It keeps the group sizes
fixed. Each time it measures the opinion variance inside the groups. The
\textbf{p-value} is the fraction of shuffles that beat the real grouping. A small
p-value (below $0.05$) means that the real communities are tighter than random.
\end{concept}

\textbf{The communities are real, but only at the fine grain.} On the 60 question
scores the Louvain grouping is much tighter than chance ($z=-6.57$, $p<0.0001$). On
the 4 topic means it is not different from random ($z=-1.26$, $p=0.108$). Louvain
groups people by their detailed answer pattern. It does not group them by their
broad position on the four topics. So the community-by-topic heatmap
(Figure~\ref{fig:heatmap}) describes each group. But the topic means do not define
the groups. We do not present them as definitions.

\textbf{Summary.} This class is agreeable across all four topics with no real
factions. The network adds four corrections to the simple reading. First, low
variance drives the apparent cohesion, so Technology and Education out-connect
Ethics and Environment even with lower means. Second, the connected core is a
behaviour type. It is the group with uniform strong agreement. It is not a
demographic or a topic camp. Third, the centrality measures disagree about who
matters, and that disagreement is itself a result. Fourth, Ethics and Environment
opinions move together (respondent-level $r=0.70$) and form one values axis.
Technology and Education are almost independent ($r=0.29$).
