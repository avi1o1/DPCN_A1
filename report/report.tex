\section*{Overview}

\lab{Task} We build a network from a class survey that covers four topics and
then study that network.

\lab{Approach} Each respondent is one node. We put an edge between two people
when their survey answers are similar. We then study the network in three ways.

\begin{dash}
  \item \emph{Structure} We measure degree, clustering, components, four
  centralities, and Louvain communities.
  \item \emph{Dynamics} We run the DeGroot opinion average and an SIS
  contagion model from the course.
  \item \emph{Null models} We compare the network with an
  Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi graph, a degree-preserving rewiring, and a label
  permutation. This helps us check whether the structure is more than chance.
\end{dash}

\lab{Headline} 51 of the 87 respondents, which is 59\%, fall into one
connected group. The other 36 do not have any strong-similarity partner.

\section{Dataset Documentation}

\lab{The raw survey} The survey has 96 responses to 60 opinion statements.
Each topic has 15 statements. The topic codes are \textbf{T} for Technology,
\textbf{E} for Education, \textbf{S} for Ethics, and \textbf{V} for
Environment. Each statement uses a five-point Likert scale along with a
``No Comments'' option.

\lab{Preparation}
\begin{dash}
  \item \emph{Likert to number} We convert Strongly Disagree to $-2$ and
  Strongly Agree to $+2$. The scale is symmetric, so $0$ really means neutral.
  The mean score then shows both direction and strength.
  \item \emph{``No Comments'' means missing} We do not treat a skipped answer as
  neutral. There are 62 blanks out of $87\times60=5220$ values, which is
  $1.19\%$. These blanks come from 19 respondents.
  \item \emph{Quality filter} We remove any respondent who answered fewer than
  half of the 60 questions. This removes 9 responses and leaves \textbf{87
  respondents}.
  \item \emph{Result} We get an $87\times60$ table of scores in $[-2,+2]$.
  Each ``No Comments'' answer stays blank. Each question also keeps its topic
  and its text.
\end{dash}

\lab{Missing values} We handle blanks in two ways depending on the calculation.
When a result has to match the graph exactly, we replace a blank with $0$
before averaging because the edge-building step also uses that rule. For
descriptive statistics, we leave the blank out of that person's average. In
practice the difference is very small. The class mean changes by at most $0.01$.
The biggest change for any one respondent is $0.23$. We keep these two uses
separate and do not compare numbers that come from different rules.

% ============================================================
\section{Pipeline Followed}

\lab{Similarity} Each respondent becomes a vector of 60 answers,
$\mathbf{x}_i\in[-2,2]^{60}$, with blanks set to $0$. We measure similarity
between two people using the cosine of the angle between their vectors.
\[
  s_{ij}=\frac{\mathbf{x}_i\cdot\mathbf{x}_j}
  {\lVert\mathbf{x}_i\rVert\,\lVert\mathbf{x}_j\rVert}\in[-1,1].
\]
Cosine similarity rewards a similar answer pattern across all 60 questions. It
does not reward only a similar average score.

\lab{Edges} We compute the full $87\times87$ cosine matrix and keep the top
\textbf{10\%} of pairs as edges. This is the 90th percentile cut.
\begin{dash}
  \item We need a hard cut. Most people in this class agree on average, so a
  loose threshold would give us an almost complete graph with very little use.
  \item This rule keeps a fixed \emph{rank} and not a fixed score. So it always
  gives exactly \textbf{375 edges}, whatever the similarity values look like.
  \item We build \textbf{five networks} with the same rule. One \emph{overall}
  network uses all 60 questions. Four others use one topic each, with 15
  questions each.
  \item Node count, edge count, and density stay the same across all five
  networks. So any difference between them comes from the wiring pattern.
  Section~\ref{sec:sens} checks the cut itself.
\end{dash}

\subsection{Structure}

\lab{Definitions} \emph{Degree} $k$ is the number of edges attached to a node.
\emph{Density} is the fraction of possible edges that are present.
\emph{Clustering} measures how often two neighbours of a node are also linked
to each other, on a scale from $0$ to $1$. A \emph{component} is a set of nodes
that can all reach one another. The largest one is the \emph{giant component}.
\emph{Modularity} $Q$ tells us how strongly the network splits into groups
compared with a random graph that keeps the same degrees.

\subsection{Centrality and communities}

\lab{Definitions} \emph{Degree} centrality counts direct connections.
\emph{Closeness} measures how short the average path from one node is to the
rest. \emph{Betweenness} counts how often a node lies on a shortest path
between two others, so it highlights bridge positions. \emph{Eigenvector}
centrality gives more weight to a node that is connected to other important
nodes. \emph{Louvain} looks for the grouping with the highest modularity.

\lab{Analysis}
\begin{dash}
  \item Degree, closeness, and eigenvector centrality mostly pick the same five
  people, which are 114, 67, 94, 105, and 110. All five score above the class
  mean on every topic. They are the people who agree strongly and consistently,
  so their 60-value vectors look similar to many others.
  \item Betweenness gives a different result. Its top node is \textbf{41}, who
  has the maximum Technology score but only an average Education score. This
  mixed profile helps that person connect sub-groups.
  \item The most connected person and the best bridge are not the same. So one
  centrality measure alone is not enough here.
  \item Louvain finds many small communities and the modularity is low at
  $Q=0.108$. Every community has a positive mean on every topic. Only one group,
  C6, goes below the class mean anywhere, and that happens only on Technology.
  These are different levels of agreement and not opposing camps.
\end{dash}

  \item Ethics ($+1.28$) and Environment ($+1.37$) have the highest average

  \item So the raw average can say that the class agrees more on Environment

\subsection{Dynamics on the network}

\lab{DeGroot} We start each respondent with their mean opinion score. At each
step, every respondent replaces that value with the average of their
neighbours' opinions, and everybody updates at the same time. The rule is
$\mathbf{x}(t{+}1)=D^{-1}A\,\mathbf{x}(t)$. Here $\mathbf{x}(t)$ is the vector
of opinion values of all respondents at step $t$. $A$ is the adjacency matrix
of the network. $D$ is the diagonal degree matrix, so each diagonal entry is
the degree of one node. The matrix $D^{-1}A$ therefore gives the neighbour
average update. The eigenvalues of this update matrix decide what happens next.
From the course, the map is stable when the relevant eigenvalue has magnitude
below $1$. The \emph{SLEM}, which is the second-largest eigenvalue modulus,
controls the speed. The algebraic connectivity is $\lambda_2=1-\mu_2$.

\sffamily Steps & \sffamily Core size & \sffamily Consensus \\
\midrule
Overall     & \gc{0.627} & \gc{0.397} & \gc{23} & 51 & 1.409 \\

The order of $\lambda_2$ matches the measured order of steps in every case.}
\label{tab:degroot}
\end{table}

\lab{Analysis}
\begin{dash}
  \item The overall network has 37 components, so there is no single class
  consensus. The 51-person core reaches \textbf{1.409} in \textbf{23 steps}.
  \item The 36 isolates never change. An isolate has no neighbour to average
  with, so its opinion at step 1000 is the same as at step 0.
  \item Convergence speed follows algebraic connectivity exactly across all five
  networks. This matches the stability rule from the course on real survey
  data.
  \item Edge weights make almost no difference. The weighted consensus is
  $1.4099$ and the unweighted one is $1.4085$. Row normalisation shrinks the
  effect of the kept similarities because they already lie in the narrow range
  from $0.85$ to $0.95$.
\end{dash}

\lab{SIS} We model a strong opinion like a contagion. An infected person can
infect each neighbour at rate $\beta$ and recover at rate $\gamma$. From the
course, the critical ratio is $(\beta/\gamma)_c = 1/\lambda_{\max}$, where
$\lambda_{\max}$ is the largest eigenvalue of the adjacency matrix. A lower
threshold means the opinion spreads more easily. This calculation uses $A$
itself and not the row-normalised $D^{-1}A$, because one infected person can
infect neighbours separately instead of averaging with them.

\subsection{Does the 10\% cut-off matter?}
\label{sec:sens}

\lab{Experiment} We apply the same rule at every quantile from $0.50$ to $0.99$
and record the structure each time.

\lab{Results}

\begin{table}[!ht]
\centering\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrr@{}}
\toprule
\sffamily\bfseries Quantile kept & \sffamily Edges & \sffamily Isolates &
\sffamily Giant component & \sffamily Clustering \\
\midrule
0.90 (used) & 375  & \rc{36} & \rc{58.6\%} & \gc{0.408} \\
0.80        & 749  & 21 & 73.6\% & 0.579 \\
0.70        & 1123 & 15 & 82.8\% & 0.643 \\
0.60        & 1497 & 13 & 85.1\% & 0.674 \\
0.50        & 1871 & \gc{8}  & \gc{90.8\%} & \rc{0.751} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{A looser cut connects most of the isolates but never all of them. At
the same time it increases clustering until the graph stops being very useful.}
\label{tab:sens}
\end{table}

\lab{Analysis}
\begin{dash}
  \item The topic order stays stable at every cut. Technology always stays above
  Environment and Education always stays above Ethics. So this order comes from
  the data and not from the 90th percentile choice.
  \item The isolates are only partly caused by the cut. Their count falls from
  36 at $q=0.90$ to 8 at $q=0.50$, but it never becomes zero. So eight people
  remain below the median similarity with everyone else in the class.
  \item A looser cut also has a cost. At $q=0.50$ the clustering rises to $0.75$
  and the graph becomes half complete. Then community detection and centrality
  do not tell us much.
  \item So $q=0.90$ is a tested compromise and not just an assumption.
\end{dash}

\section{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:results}

\lab{What the network says about class opinions} This class is broadly
agreeable. The mean opinion is positive on all four topics and the raw data
does not show opposing camps. The network still reveals structure that the
averages do not show clearly.

\lab{Finding 1. No global consensus and the drop-outs are the moderates}
\begin{dash}
  \item Under DeGroot the 51-person core agrees at $1.409$ while the 36 isolates
  never move.
  \item The core started at a mean of $1.309$. The isolates started at $0.872$.
  \item So the process removes the more moderate half of the class. This is a
  structural effect of the construction, not something the averaging model
  causes.
\end{dash}

\lab{Finding 2. Lower variance gives stronger cohesion} Technology and Education
look better connected than Ethics and Environment even though their mean scores
are lower. This happens because their opinions are less spread out. Raw
agreement and social cohesion are not the same thing.

\lab{Finding 3. More fragmented topics have lower SIS thresholds}

\begin{table}[!ht]
\centering\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrr@{}}
\toprule
\sffamily\bfseries Network & \sffamily $\lambda_{\max}$ &
\sffamily $1/\lambda_{\max}$ & \sffamily Giant size & \sffamily Giant density \\
\midrule
Environment & \gc{23.92} & \gc{0.0418} & \rc{44} & \gc{0.395} \\
Ethics      & 22.45 & 0.0445 & 45 & 0.378 \\
Overall     & 22.06 & 0.0453 & 51 & 0.294 \\
Technology  & 18.17 & 0.0550 & \gc{65} & 0.179 \\
Education   & \rc{17.74} & \rc{0.0564} & 63 & \rc{0.191} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Epidemic thresholds. This order is the reverse of the connectivity
order in Table~\ref{tab:struct}. The most broken topic spreads most easily.}
\label{tab:epi}
\end{table}

\begin{dash}
  \item Environment and Ethics have the lowest thresholds at $0.0418$ and
  $0.0445$, yet they are the least connected topics.
  \item The edge count is fixed at 375, so a smaller giant component packs almost
  the same edges into fewer people. Environment puts 374 edges into 44
  respondents. Technology spreads 373 across 65.
  \item A denser core raises $\lambda_{\max}$, which lowers the threshold. The
  standard bound $\langle k\rangle \le \lambda_{\max} \le k_{\max}$ applied inside
  the giant component predicts this ordering directly.
  \item Fragmentation and easy spread are not opposites here. This is a real
  property of the data and also a warning about the fixed-edge method.
\end{dash}

\lab{Finding 4. The structure sits in the degree sequence} A null model asks
whether a random network with no opinion structure would give the same result.
The z-score is $z=(\text{real}-\text{null mean})/\text{null s.d.}$. When
$|z|<2$, the real value looks close to random.

\begin{table}[!ht]
\centering\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrrr@{}}
\toprule
\sffamily\bfseries Statistic & \sffamily Real & \sffamily ER mean &
\sffamily $z$ & \sffamily Rewired mean & \sffamily $z$ \\
\midrule
Average clustering  & 0.408 & 0.098 & \rc{$+27.2$} & 0.391 & \gc{$+1.6$} \\
Giant fraction      & 0.586 & 1.000 & \rc{$-361.7$} & 0.586 & \gc{$0.0$} \\

\end{tabular}
\caption{The real network is compared with 500 Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi graphs of the
same density and 500 degree-preserving rewirings of itself. Green marks a
z-score inside the random band.}
\label{tab:null}
\end{table}

\begin{dash}
  \item Against 500 ER graphs the network is unusual on every measure, with a
  clustering z-score of $+27$ and a giant-fraction z-score of $-362$.
  \item Against 500 degree-preserving rewirings it is ordinary. Clustering, giant
  component, and modularity all fall inside $|z|<2$.
  \item So once each person's edge count is fixed, chance supplies the rest of
  the structure.
  \item For this construction that is the same statement as ``the connected core
  is the group that agrees with everything''. Agreement level sets the degree, and
  after the degrees are fixed nothing is left to explain. This gives the reason
  for the structural analysis rather than weakening it.
\end{dash}

  \label{fig:null}

\lab{Finding 5. The communities are real, but only at the fine level} A
permutation test shuffles the community labels 10{,}000 times while keeping the
group sizes fixed. It then measures the opinion variance inside the groups. The
p-value is the fraction of shuffles that do better than the real grouping.

\begin{table}[!ht]
\centering\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrr@{}}
\toprule
\sffamily\bfseries Features tested & \sffamily Observed & \sffamily Null mean &
\sffamily $z$ & \sffamily $p$ \\
\midrule
All 60 question scores & 0.4116 & 0.4592 & \gc{$-6.57$} & \gc{$<0.0001$} \\
4 topic means          & 0.0791 & 0.0839 & \rc{$-1.26$} & \rc{$0.108$} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{The same communities pass when we test the answers used to build the
graph and fail when we test only the coarser topic averages.}
\label{tab:perm}
\end{table}

\begin{dash}
  \item On the 60 question scores the Louvain grouping is far tighter than chance
  ($z=-6.57$, $p<0.0001$).
  \item On the 4 topic means it is not distinguishable from random ($z=-1.26$,
  $p=0.108$).
  \item Louvain groups people by their detailed answer pattern, not by their
  broad position on the four topics. Two people in one community can differ on
  topic averages while answering the individual questions alike.
  \item The community-by-topic comparison in Figure~\ref{fig:heatmap} therefore
  \emph{describes} each group but
  does not \emph{define} it, and is not presented as a definition.
\end{dash}

\begin{figure}[!ht]

\lab{In summary}
\begin{dash}
  \item The class is agreeable across all four topics, with no real factions.
  \item Low variance drives the apparent cohesion, so Technology and Education
  out-connect Ethics and Environment even with lower means.
  \item The connected core is a behaviour type, the uniform strong-agreement
  answerers, not a demographic or a topic camp.
  \item Centrality measures disagree about who matters, and that disagreement is
  itself a result.
  \item Ethics and Environment move together at a respondent-level $r=0.70$ and
  form one values axis. Technology and Education are nearly independent at
  $r=0.29$.
\end{dash}
